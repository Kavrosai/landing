Posture

Fail closed, on purpose: when the gate can't be verified

What the governed path does when evidence is missing — refusal, the baseline floor, suspension, and the halt. Including what this posture does not claim.

8 min read • November 2026

A security control is only as good as what it does when it can't be sure.

Every control has a failure mode. The architectural question is not whether it will sometimes fail to verify something — a policy can't be fetched, a trust check can't complete, evidence goes stale at exactly the wrong moment. The question is which direction the failure faces: fail open — proceed, log, hope a human notices later — or fail closed — refuse, record, ask.

Services that proxy forty thousand requests a minute tend to fail open, because failing closed threatens availability. That's a defensible trade for a monitoring surface. It is not a defensible trade for a gate. This post states Kavros's default face on each governed path, and then — because a posture post that stops before naming its own blind spots is marketing, not engineering — what the posture does not claim.

The request path defaults to refusal

On the governed path in your VPC, a request is evaluated before it can move: policy evaluation happens inside the enclave, DLP inspection runs before forward, and the data plane fails closed before anything forwards. A request that cannot be evaluated — missing evidence, invalid evidence, an unverifiable state — isn't put on the wire with a warning label. It doesn't proceed.

Concretely, on each failure the gate answers with refusal:

  • A quote that fails any check is refused, never forwarded. Attestation of the enclave is verified — valid signature path, current measurement, exact match against the deployment's approved enclave measurement — before any byte is forwarded.
  • Unapproved images get no trust at all. The runtime refuses to trust a quote from any other enclave image. Measurements are registered and approved before deployment, automatically on match and through administrator review whenever anything differs — an observed measurement can never promote itself to "known good" by showing up.
  • A tampered or unsigned policy doesn't widen anything. Policies are Ed25519-signed in the control plane and verified inside the enclave at enforcement time; a policy that can't be verified falls back to the baked-in baseline floor, not to "whatever the process asked for."

When what you approved no longer exists

Refusal at request time still leaves a quieter question: what happens when nothing in the request looks wrong, but the thing underneath changed? That's workflow drift, and it gets two mechanisms with deliberately different teeth — capability changes are flagged at the publish and schedule gates with a human confirming on the record, while source schema drift suspends the capability outright: every call refused before the database sees a query, a critical incident opened with the reason attached. Part 2 of the Workflows series describes both as they behaved in a live rehearsal, including the sentence we first got wrong about tier 1 and had to fix in our own copy.

The intentional stop

Fail-closed posture isn't only about the system refusing when it is in doubt. It's also about the operator being able to stop it when they are. An out-of-process halt revokes active credential leases and refuses every request from a halted workload — quoting the operator's stated reason — until an administrator resumes it. No application redeploy, no key scavenger hunt, no "hope it behaves." The refusal is quotable: it doesn't lock you out, it tells you why, on the record, until a human says otherwise.

What this posture does not claim

  • Fail closed on covered paths, not everywhere. A coverage view shows every provider-bound model request Kavros observed, which governed surface sent it, and — explicitly — what Kavros cannot see. A clean panel is never presented as a no-bypass claim. Any vendor who shows you a spotless dashboard without that section is showing you a claim of perfection; reject it.
  • Attestation covers the governed egress gateway — not the model, not every tool. Hardware attestation evidences the enclave that evaluates egress; other tool execution is enforced at the application layer with signed manifests, grants, and DLP. Saying "everything runs in the enclave" would be false and we don't.
  • Per-request cryptographic binding is roadmap, not shipped. Today's attestation quotes evidence the enclave's measured code and state; binding a quote to the exact request bytes is roadmap (F-04). The evidence of what happened lives in the hash-chained decision records either way — but the boundary between the two is the boundary, and we'd rather own it than blur it.
  • Evidence is not compliance. Refusals and signature chaining produce verifiable records for auditors; they don't make an organization automatically compliant, and they don't certify the correctness of a policy — approval proves a human reviewed scope, not that the scope is right.

The question to keep asking

Ask any layer that touches your agents one lazy question: when you can't verify your evidence, what do you do? If the answer is "proceed and log," you have a recording device. Useful. Different product.

If the answer is a flowchart nobody can quote, ask the sharper version: which paths fail open — and is there a view that says so? There should be. Ours does, in coverage terms of what it can't see, because the alternative to admitting the boundary is pretending it's wider than it is.
